ABSTRACT 

Quantifying animal behavior often requires segmenting continuous actions into discrete, interpretable states, yet most automated pipelines infer actions from keypoint dynamics and are limited by keypoint tracking quality. Here we present FERAL (Feature Extrac-tion for Recognition of Animal Locomotion), a supervised video-understanding toolkit that maps raw video directly to frame-level behavioral labels, bypassing the keypoint-extraction and pose-classification stages of conventional pipelines. Across benchmarks, FERAL matches or exceeds state-of-the-art pose- and video-based baselines. On the CalMS21 mouse social-interaction benchmark, it exceeds Google’s VideoPrism using only a quarter of the training data. FERAL generalizes across species (apes, zebras, mice, ants, flies, and nematodes), recording conditions, and levels of organization, from single animals to social interactions and colony-scale collective behavior. Released as a user-friendly, open-source package, it integrates with existing analysis pipelines. By enabling scalable, species-agnostic behavioral quantification directly from raw video, FERAL broadens the experimental paradigms available for studying animal behavior.

## INTRODUCTION 

Animal behavior unfolds continuously in time, yet is often parsed into discrete, human-2

interpreted actions [1–4]. Since the earliest days of ethology, researchers have identified 3

these actions through naturalistic observation and manual annotation [5, 6], establishing the 4

foundation of modern behavioral science [3, 7, 8]. Such analyses have been essential across 5

disciplines, informing research in ecology [9], neuroscience [10], neurodegenerative disease 6

modeling, and drug discovery for disorders with observable behavioral phenotypes. 7

The advent of videotaping in the mid-1980s enabled the quantification of behavior with 8

greater temporal resolution than possible by direct observation alone. Manual annotation 9

remains the most versatile method for quantifying recorded behavior, adapting to scene com-10 

plexities that range from controlled arenas to field recordings. However, manual annotation is 11 

labor-intensive and subjective, and remains the main bottleneck in scaling behavioral research. 12 

This burden can be alleviated either by reducing the per-frame labeling cost or by shifting it 13 

to the training stage of a classifier that generalizes across recordings [11–13]. Advances in 14 

computer vision have partially mitigated this limitation through markerless tracking tools 15

Pose-based pipelines have advanced two complementary approaches: unsupervised discovery 30

while the latter is ideal for scaling up the temporal segmentation of specific behaviors when 35 

keypoint dynamics are sufficient for reliable detection. 36 

The performance of these approaches, however, depends on the confidence of keypoint 37 

detection, and the skeletonized representations they require discard contextual information 38 

that can be essential for accurate behavioral interpretation. As the complexity of behavioral 39 

organization increases, such as in social or collective dynamics, pose-based pipelines become 40 

increasingly demanding, requiring multi-animal tracking and extensive post-tracking curation 41

alongside or independently of existing pose-based frameworks, providing a single, integrated 60 

tool for behavioral segmentation. 61 

Leveraging a pretrained video foundation model [32, 33], FERAL integrates motion and 62 

visual cues within a unified architecture. Trained directly on videos aligned with user-63 

provided annotations, it detects discrete actions and outputs frame-level behavioral time-64 

series (i.e., segmentations over a user-supplied ethogram, the list of behavior categories of 65 

interest) that reliably capture discrete animal behavior. 66

## RESULTS Direct video-to-behavior mapping with FERAL 80 

FERAL provides a user-friendly workflow that takes raw videos and behavioral annotations 81 

as inputs, fine-tunes an open-source video-understanding model, and outputs frame-level 82 

behavioral labels (ethograms). To ensure reproducibility across laboratories and recording 83 

setups, the pipeline standardizes both inputs (videos and labels) into a common format. 84 

The first stage is video preprocessing . Because video-understanding models operate on 85 

relatively low-resolution inputs (e.g., by default 256 × 256 pixels for the V-JEPA 2 backbone), 86 

input videos must be resized and re-encoded into seekable formats to enable efficient frame 87 

sampling [32, 34]. Within FERAL, this feature operates on Linux and Windows operating 88 

systems to automate this process, producing standardized inputs regardless of the original 89 

recording format ( Figure 1c ). 90 

The second stage, label preparation , converts behavioral annotations from diverse software 91

Because full-length video recordings cannot be processed in a single pass due to computa-97

After evaluating several potential backbones, we adopted V-JEPA 2 for the FERAL default 103 

configuration [32]. V-JEPA 2 is a foundation model from Meta Fundamental AI Research 104 

(see Methods). Pretrained on over one million hours of internet video with a self-supervised 105 

masked-prediction objective, V-JEPA 2 yields spatiotemporal embeddings that transfer to 106 

downstream video-understanding tasks. Within FERAL, we fine-tune the upper twelve of 107 

twenty-four transformer layers using user-supplied videos and labels, aligning pretrained 108 

representations with the specific demands of behavioral segmentation rather than retraining 109 

from scratch [38] ( Figure 1d ). 110 

To generate frame-level predictions, FERAL extends the encoder with an attention-based 111 

pooling module and a classification head. Each video chunk is represented as a sequence of 112 

spatiotemporal tokens that are integrated by a transformer, then compressed via attention 113 

pooling into temporally aligned embeddings. These embeddings are normalized and linearly 114 

projected into class logits, producing frame-wise probabilities that are ensembled across 115 

overlapping segments. 116

## FERAL outperforms state-of-the-art methods across benchmarks 122 

To evaluate FERAL’s precision and robustness relative to existing approaches, we benchmarked 123 

its performance on established datasets that provide raw videos aligned with frame-level 124 

behavioral annotations. The first, the Caltech Mouse Social Interactions (CalMS21) dataset 125 

[39], contains recordings of freely behaving mice ( Mus musculus ) engaged in resident–intruder 126 

assays, paired with both tracked poses and expert behavioral labels ( Figure 2a ). 127 

Originally introduced as a community challenge for the classification of social behavior, the 128 

CalMS21 dataset included multiple baseline models and public submissions. We compared 129 

FERAL against a suite of alternative pose- and video-based approaches using mean average 130 

precision (mAP), the official metric of the original challenge. A random classifier would get 131 

13.5% mAP while a perfect one would achieve 100% mAP. The strongest released baseline, 132 

in addition to the labeled behavioral dataset, employed self-supervised pretraining on large 133 

sets of unlabeled pose trajectories [39]. We also report the Competition Top-1 entry from the 134 

official leaderboard, results from Google’s VideoPrism paper [33], Task Programming [40], 135 

VJEPA-H [31] and TRACE [41]. To accommodate compute and runtime limitations of more 136 

users and make our tool more broadly accessible, we are providing and benchmarking three 137 

distinct configurations for FERAL: lite , default , and max . We discuss GPU VRAM requirements, 138 

runtimes, and performance for each of these configurations in the Methods section. 139 

FERAL default reached 94.2% mAP on CalMS21 ( Figure 2b-c, Supplementary Video 1 ), 140 

exceeding the Sun et al. 2021 [39] self-supervised pose-pretraining baseline, the MABe 2021 141 

challenge Top-1, and results from the VideoPrism paper [33]; all the numbers are mAP under 142

## FERAL captures the temporal structure and visual appearance of behaviors 238 

To assess FERAL’s ability to generalize across species and recording modalities, we applied 239 

it to two datasets, both acquired in laboratory conditions, representing distinct levels of 240 

behavioral organization and scene complexity. 241 

Single-animal behavior in C. elegans nematodes. This dataset comprises recordings 242 

of freely moving C. elegans nematodes performing four canonical locomotor behaviors: for-243 

ward crawling, reverse crawling, turning, and pausing ( Figure 3a ) [44–48]. Unlike other 244 

datasets used in this study, these behavioral labels were not manually annotated; instead, 245 

each frame was automatically assigned to a locomotory state using a rule-based heuristic 246 

pipeline adapted from prior work [48, 49]. In this pipeline, worms were segmented from 247 

the background, centroid positions were extracted, head–tail orientation was inferred from 248 

midline dynamics, and locomotor state was determined using speed thresholds, direction 249 

of motion, and self-intersection criteria. This is therefore a benchmark of model agreement 250

## DISCUSSION 

The quantitative study of animal behavior has advanced rapidly with the integration of 445 

computer vision and deep learning [3, 7]. Existing methods have excelled at answering 446 

where animals and their body parts are over time, yet determining what actions animals 447 

are performing remains challenging, especially in complex environments. This limitation 448 

constrains discovery across the fields of ethology, neuroscience, and ecology [8, 12], where 449 

understanding behavior and its structure is essential. 450 

FERAL addresses this gap by reframing behavioral quantification as a video-understanding 451 

problem. Instead of relying on intermediate abstractions such as pose or trajectory, FERAL 452 

26

maps raw video directly to frame-level behavioral labels, capturing both the temporal and 453 

visual appearance of animal actions. It achieves high performance across datasets, detecting 454 

discrete actions with high precision on a wide range of scene complexities ( Figure 5f-g ). 455 

This generalization is enabled by its design. Its foundation-model backbone allows robust 456 

fine-tuning even with limited behavioral annotations [32]. 457 

FERAL sits within a broader landscape of automated behavioral quantification. The closest 458 

pixel-input precedent is DeepEthogram [24], which performs supervised classification of 459 

behavior directly from raw video using a two-stream convolutional neural network (CNN). 460 

FERAL extends this approach with a fine-tuned video foundation model and substantially 461 

improves accuracy on five of six DeepEthogram datasets. For unsupervised behavioral 462 

discovery — uncovering categories not specified by a human annotator — methods such as 463 

MoSeq [43], keypoint-MoSeq [19], B-SOiD [20], and VAME [21] remain the appropriate tools. 464 

FERAL is complementary to, not a substitute for, this line of work. Recent video-foundation-465 

model approaches for animal behavior — DAP [25], ChimpVLM [26], and PriVi [27] — target 466 

behaviors only for primates. FERAL is a general-purpose drop-in supervised pipeline that 467 

runs across species with frame-level labels alone. 468 

FERAL operates on user-supplied behaviors and emits per-frame predictions constrained 469 

by this list of behaviors. It does not autonomously discover new behavioral categories. 470 

Despite these limitations, FERAL is designed for broad accessibility: it is an open-source, 471 

freely available toolkit ( getferal.ai ) that includes comprehensive documentation, tutorials, 472 

and benchmark datasets, lowering the technical barrier for users across disciplines. Its modular 473 

architecture supports all stages of analysis, from preprocessing and training to inference, and 474 

can be deployed locally on desktop computers, on high-performance clusters, or through 475 

cloud-based GPU services such as Google Colab Pro and RunPod. 476 

FERAL integrates with existing pipelines. By default, FERAL focuses on frame-level 477 

segmentation and does not assign persistent identities. Nevertheless, it can be paired with 478

FERAL is a supervised classifier: it can only predict behaviors present in its training labels, 483 

and frames whose behavior is absent from the schema are routed to the nearest semantic 484 

neighbor or to background rather than discovered as new; behavioral discovery remains the 485 

domain of unsupervised methods such as MoSeq [43], keypoint-MoSeq [19], and B-SOiD 486 

[20]. Additionally, FERAL outputs categorical labels, not continuous kinematic variables 487 

(joint angles, gait phase, head orientation); pose-tracking pipelines such as DeepLabCut [14], 488 

SLEAP [15], and LightningPose [16] remain the standard approach for postural readouts, and 489 

the cleanest analyses will often combine them with FERAL (pose for kinematics, FERAL for 490 

behavioral bouts). 491 

Importantly, FERAL classifies the scene rather than the individuals in it: in pair and social 492 

settings the model does not assign actor–recipient identity. In scenarios in which the animal of 493 

interest occupies only a small fraction of the frame (single-worm assays, single-zebra vigilance, 494 

multi-ape camera-trap clips), FERAL requires an upstream tracker or detector to produce 495 

animal-centered crops. This requirement is shared with most supervised pose-based and 496 

pixel-based classifiers. 497 

FERAL shifts annotation effort to the training stage rather than eliminating it: a moderate 498 

annotation budget ( ∼50% of bouts) recovers most of the benefit for common behaviors, but 499 

rare-class detection remains bound by the labeling effort directed at that specific class. 500 

By combining strong performance with user-friendly design, FERAL establishes a robust 501 

foundation for behavioral segmentation across species and experimental paradigms. FERAL 502 

code and documentation are available at getferal.ai (Zenodo DOI to be assigned). 503

Chunking strategy 887 

Transformer-based video encoders compute pairwise attention across all spatiotemporal 888 

tokens, causing computational complexity to scale quadratically with the number of input 889 

tokens. As a result, processing entire behavioral recordings in a single pass is infeasible. 890 

Consequently, FERAL divides each video into overlapping segments, or chunks , of fixed 891 

length before processing. Each chunk comprises 64 consecutive frames, resized to a common 892 

resolution of 256 × 256 pixels by default. 893 

To capture fine-grained behavioral dynamics, consecutive chunks overlap by 50% (i.e., 894 

stride = 32 frames). This design ensures that short behaviors spanning chunk boundaries 895 

remain fully visible within at least one receptive field. During training, overlapping windows 896 

also increase the effective number of training samples, further improving quality. 897 

During inference, as the model outputs per-frame predictions for each chunk, we employ a 898 

unified post-processing pipeline to gather the final frame-level predictions. If a frame received 899 

multiple predictions, we averaged the corresponding class probabilities. For frames without 900 

a direct prediction, we linearly interpolated probabilities from the two nearest frames with 901 

predictions. This approach effectively ensembles the model’s own local predictions. 902

## Metrics 1059 

Frame-level mean average precision (mAP) is computed with 1060 

sklearn.metrics.average_precision_score on per-class softmax probabilities for single-1061 

label datasets and per-class sigmoid probabilities for multi-label datasets. Throughout this 1062 

manuscript, mAP refers to the PASCAL-VOC convention: per-class average precision averaged 1063 

across foreground classes (the “other” class is excluded from the macro-average but retained 1064 

as a negative for the foreground APs). We additionally report macro F1 — the per-class 1065 

F1 averaged across foreground classes. For multi-label tasks (e.g., MABe22) we binarize 1066 

predictions using a fixed sigmoid threshold of 0.85 per class. For the single-label setting, 1067 

we count a frame as a predicted positive when its logit is the highest among all classes, and 1068 

negative otherwise. We also report accuracy, defined as the proportion of correctly classified 1069 

frames. For the multi-label setting, we call a frame correctly classified when predictions for 1070 

all classes match the target. The performance metrics of FERAL across all benchmark datasets 1071 

using the same default configuration are reported in Table 13. 1072 

Metric rationale 1073 

Accuracy was not used as the primary metric, since behavioral datasets typically exhibit 1074 

substantial class imbalance. Datasets often include a frequent “other” class, while biologically 1075 

interesting events are relatively rare (often <1% of frames). In such settings, accuracy overesti-1076 

mates performance by rewarding majority-class predictions. We therefore report mAP, which 1077 

offers a more sensitive measure of per-class discrimination across thresholds, with macro 1078 

F1, precision, and recall providing a complementary view. mAP is computed on the raw 1079 

per-class probabilities described above; the macro F1 score is computed after thresholding 1080

Model selection 1085 

For all runs, we train the model on the full training dataset and then evaluate it on the held-out 1086 

test dataset. We do not perform checkpoint selection on a validation split (standard vs EMA, 1087 

and across epochs), because many of the datasets were small enough that splitting the training 1088 

set into train and validation either harmed performance by making the training set too small 1089 

or made the validation split uninformative when it was too small and produced noisy results. 1090 

This was especially challenging for datasets where some behaviors were present in only a few 1091 

videos, as the validation set needed to include all behaviors of interest to remain informative. 1092 

Therefore, we decided to consistently select the non-EMA checkpoint from the last epoch. 1093 

We acknowledge that this approach may introduce mild test-data leakage, since we select 1094 

hyperparameters based on test-set performance. However, we use a single set of hyperparam-1095 

eters across all datasets to avoid overoptimizing for any individual dataset. 1096

## Engineering and deployment 1106 

Video preprocessing and seekability. Training requires fast access to arbitrary frames in the 1107 

video. Some common codecs support only sequential reads and do not allow frame-level 1108 

seeking, so re-encoding is often necessary. Pre-resizing can also help with HD videos, as 1109 

decoding large videos may be too slow compared with GPU computation speed. FERAL 1110 

includes a cross-platform utility that converts input videos to seekable formats and resizes 1111 

them to 256 × 256 px. If source videos are not high resolution and are already seekable, users 1112 

may skip re-encoding; resizing will then be performed dynamically during training. 1113 

Hugging Face integration. To ensure modularity and ease of extension, FERAL leverages 1114 

the Hugging Face transformers library [75, 76] for model management. This abstraction 1115 

simplifies switching between backbones and streamlines installation compared to earlier 1116 

architectures (e.g., InternVideo2), which relied on bespoke code and complex dependencies. 1117 

Training visualization and monitoring. FERAL integrates with Weights & Biases (W&B) 1118 

[74] for experiment tracking, allowing users to monitor training, validation, and test per-1119 

formance. Logs are stored in the cloud, enabling training across multiple servers while 1120 

maintaining a unified analysis interface. FERAL comes with a community W&B workspace 1121 

and a public API key in default_config.yaml to lower the onboarding burden for users 1122 

without a W&B account; a --no-wandb flag is provided for fully offline operation, and users 1123 

can connect their own W&B accounts at any time. 1124 

Deployment and compute requirements. FERAL default requires 24GB of VRAM for 1125 

training, which fits on high-end consumer GPUs. With gradient checkpointing enabled, the 1126 

default configuration can also fit within 8GB of VRAM, albeit with longer training times. 1127 

The Lite version has an even smaller VRAM footprint, delivers slightly lower performance 1128 

than the default model, but provides nearly 2 × faster training. For users without access to 1129 

GPU hardware, FERAL provides step-by-step deployment guides for GPU cloud platforms 1130 

(e.g., RunPod) as well as a Google Colab notebook, enabling full training runs at low cost and 1131
