# Edge Detection Framework Utilizing SOT-MTJ Bit-Cell Arrays 

Kushagra Singh, Debasis Das 

Dept. of Electrical and Electronics Engineering BITS Pilani, K K Birla Goa Campus 

Goa, India, 403726 Email: f20231210,debasisdas@goa.bits-pilani.ac.in 

Abstract —Traditional edge detection algorithms, foundational to computer vision, face significant challenges in energy ef-ficiency and processing latency on conventional CMOS-based hardware. Existing algorithms, such as Canny, are computa-tionally expensive, posing challenges in resource-constrained hardware where energy efficiency and low latency are critical. This study introduces a novel, hardware-efficient algorithm that leverages the intrinsic characteristics of magnetic tunnel junction (MTJ) devices. We present a detailed device-level analysis of an MTJ-based system for edge detection, outlining its operational cycles, including write, read, and reset methods. The algorithm’s efficacy is evaluated against the standard Canny edge detection method. We provide a quantitative performance analysis, including metrics such as energy consumption and latency, which demonstrates that our proposed spintronics-based approach offers a promising solution for achieving low-power, high-speed image processing. 

Index Terms —MTJs, Spintronics, Edge Detection, Image Processing, Landau-Lifshitz-Gilbert-Slonczewski (LLGS), spin-orbit torques (SOTs), In-Memory Computing. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Edge detection is a cornerstone of digital image processing and computer vision, serving as the initial step for higher-level tasks such as object recognition, image segmentation, and scene understanding [1]–[4]. Mainstream algorithms, such as the Canny edge detector, have proven effective but are inherently computationally intensive [2]. This computational burden, however, is particularly problematic for resource-constrained edge devices, where the demand for low power consumption and minimal processing latency is paramount. This is largely due to the Von-Neumann bottleneck, which forces a constant, time-consuming data transfer between the separate processing unit and memory, limiting the parallelism and efficiency necessary for data-intensive tasks like image processing. As CMOS scaling approaches its physical limits, these issues are exacerbated, motivating the exploration of alternative computing paradigms [5]. To overcome this funda-mental architectural limitation, emerging non-volatile mem-ory (eNVM) devices-such as resistive RAM (ReRAM) [6], ferroelectric memories [7], and spintronic [8] devices are being explored. Apart from storing the memory bits, these de-vices offer in-memory computing, where computational tasks are performed directly within the memory array itself [9]– [11]. This approach drastically reduces data movement, al-

Among various eNVM devices, spintronics devices are particularly promising as they offer high speed, excellent endurance, low static power consumption, and compatibility with existing CMOS technology [12]. The continued demand for high-speed, low-power, and highly scalable non-volatile memories has driven intense research into magnetoresistive random-access memory (MRAM). The fundamental building block of MRAM is the MTJ, where a thin insulating barrier is sandwiched between two ferromagnetic layers [13], [14]. Data is stored by controlling the relative magnetic alignment of these layers. In this work, we investigate the device physics of MTJs to realize a novel hardware-friendly image edge detection algorithm. Our primary objective is to exploit the switching characteristics of the MTJs, to achieve a low-latency and energy-efficient edge detector. We perform numerical simu-lations of the MTJ’s magnetization dynamics to demonstrate its utility as a reconfigurable kernel for image processing. The approach is validated by comparing its performance against the industry-standard Canny edge detection algorithm, thereby demonstrating its potential as a next-generation solu-tion for efficient in-sensor or in-memory image processing. II. D EVICE PHYSICS AND OPERATION 

The core of our device is the MTJ, a three-layer stack consisting of an oxide layer, sandwiched between two ferromagnet (FM) layers. The first FM layer, referred to as the pinned layer (PL), has a magnetization direction fixed by an exchange bias to an adjacent antiferromagnetic layer. In contrast, the second FM layer, the free layer (FL), is designed with uniaxial anisotropy, allowing its magnetization to be switched between two stable states by an external stimulus. The device exhibits two distinct resistance states: a low-resistance state ( RP) when the magnetizations of the FL and PL are aligned in parallel (P), and a high-resistance state (RAP ) when they are in anti-parallel (AP) orientation [15]. This difference in resistances allows the MTJ to function as a non-volatile memory element by associating each resistance state with a binary value (e.g., “0” or “1”). The quality of the device is quantified by the tunnel magnetoresistance (TMR) ratio, which is given as TMR= (R AP − RP) /RP. The

magnetization of the free layer is altered by passing a spin-polarized current through the device. There are two basic methods to perform this operation. First, there is the spin-transfer torque (STT), where the spin angular momentum from the pinned layer is transferred to the free layer via spin-polarized electrons, causing its magnetization to switch. However, a key disadvantage of STT is that the switching current must pass directly through the tunnel barrier, which can degrade the barrier over time and reduce the device’s endurance. Furthermore, the shared read and write current path in STT can lead to unwanted bit flips during read operations, compromising data integrity. This challenge led to the development of spin-orbit torque (SOT), where the spin current is generated in a separate heavy metal layer grown next to the FL, and the spin torque is applied in a plane perpendicular to the current [16]. This method is advantageous because it separates the read and write current paths, preventing degradation of the tunnel barrier and leading to faster, more energy-efficient switching. Fig 1(a) illustrates the schematic of an SOT-MTJ device, where the FL is attached to a HM layer. Magnetization of the PL layer is fixed along the +z-direction, while the magnetiza-tion of the FL is switched by SOT. When an electric current is passed through the HM layer along the +x-direction, the electron spins get polarized along the ±z-direction, where the 

+z-direction (-z-direction) spins may accumulate on the top (bottom) surface of the HM layer. This leads to a spin current flowing into the FL, and eventually switching the magne-tization. In this section, we demonstrate the method for image edge detection and compute the corresponding latency and energy consumption. Most color images are composed of three channels, Red, Green, and Blue (RGB), where the pixels for each channel are arranged in a two-dimensional grid. Edge detection is performed exclusively on single-channel grayscale images; therefore, a preprocessing step is required for any color image. Initially, the color image is split into its RGB channels, as shown in Fig. 2(a). To convert it to a single-channel grayscale image, one can either compute the mean of the three channels or use the weighted formula 0.3*R+0.59*G+0.11*B, where R, G, and B represent the respective pixel values. We use the latter method in our simulation, as illustrated in Fig. 2(a). The pixel values of grayscale images are 8-bit unsigned integers, which can range from 0 to 255. For instance, a sample of 2 ×2 pixel grid, as shown in Fig. 2(a), represents these unsigned integer values. Subsequently, each pixel’s integer value is converted into an 8-bit unsigned binary number, with the most significant bit (MSB) on the left and the least significant bit (LSB) on the right. This approach can be applied to the entire grayscale image, converting it into eight separate bit channels, ranging from the MSB to the LSB, as depicted in Fig. 2(b). It is noteworthy that the MSB contains the maximum amount of image information, which progressively decreases toward the LSB. Consequently, edge detection can be effectively performed using either the MSB alone or a combination of the MSB and Bit 6. For the image shown in Fig. 2(a), we use only the MSB plane, which is a binary image where the pixel values are either ‘1’ or ‘0’. Next, we implement a 3 ×3 kernel with the SOT-MTJ bitcells and convolve it over the MSB image with a stride of 1, as shown in Fig. 3(a) [17]. The convolution process involves a three-step cycle-write , read , and reset for each MTJs corresponding to each pixel. To begin, we apply the pixel values to the WWL for 2.5 ns to perform the write 

operation. If all nine pixel values are ‘1’, every MTJ will switch to the P-state. Conversely, if all pixel values are ‘0’, none of the MTJs will switch and they will remain in the AP-state. If a subset of the nine pixels has a value of ‘1’, only the corresponding MTJs will switch. For the subsequent 1 ns, the read operation is performed by simultaneously applying a small voltage across all the MTJs while deactivating the WWL and WBL and activating the RBL. There are three possible outcomes: (i) If all nine pixel values applied during the write operation were ‘1’, all nine MTJs switch to the P-state. This results in a current of IP flowing through each individual MTJ, yielding a total current of 9 IP. (ii) If all nine pixel values were ‘0’, none of the MTJs switch, resulting in a total read current of 9 IAP . (iii) If a subset of the pixels were ‘1’, the corresponding MTJs switch, leading to a read current that falls between 9 IP and 9 IAP , as shown in Fig. 3(b). For the two extreme cases, where all nine pixels are identical, indicating no variation, we conclude no edge is detected. Consequently, a pixel value of ‘0’ can be assigned to the edge detection images. Conversely, in the third case, where the read current is between 9 IP and 9 IAP , a pixel variation is present, indicating an edge in the binary image. Therefore, a pixel value of ‘1’ can be assigned to the edge detection image. This process effectively converts the input image into an edge detection image, as depicted in Fig. 3(c). II, for the write , read ,and reset energy for a single SOT-MTJ, the total energy consumption for detecting the edges of the given MSB image (1024 ×679 pixels) is calculated as 0.16 μJ, with a latency of 4 ms. We have demonstrated a low-energy, hardware-friendly ap-proach for image edge detection. Unlike the computationally intensive preprocessing of the Canny algorithm, our method relies on converting color images to grayscale and then splitting them across 8 bits-a process that can be efficiently implemented with an analog-to-digital converter. Through numerical simulation, we show that SOT-MTJs can be ef-fectively used as a kernel to detect image edges, consuming only a few μJ of energy and incurring a delay of a few ms. With such low latency and energy consumption, our approach is promising for use in a range of applications, including the analysis of complex datasets like medical images. REFERENCES [1] J.
